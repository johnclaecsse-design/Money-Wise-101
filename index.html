The 5 ideas in 60 seconds

What investing actually is

Investing means putting money into something with the expectation that it will be worth more later — in exchange for taking on the risk that it might instead be worth less. That's the whole trade-off. Saving money in a bank account avoids that risk but barely grows; investing accepts risk in exchange for the chance of real growth.

You're not investing in a company's logo or its product — you're investing in a legal claim on either its future profits (a stock) or its promise to repay a loan with interest (a bond). Everything below is a variation on one of those two ideas.

Stocks

A stock (or "share") is a small ownership stake in a company. If a company has 1,000,000 shares and you own 1,000 of them, you own 0.1% of that company. As a part-owner, your shares tend to become more valuable when the company grows its profits — and less valuable when it doesn't, or when investors lose confidence in it.

This is why stock prices move constantly: they're not a fixed price tag, they're a live, ongoing guess by everyone buying and selling about what the company is worth right now. That guess can be wrong in both directions, which is what the Time advantage section's case study is actually about.

Bonds

A bond works differently: instead of buying part-ownership, you're lending money — to a company or a government — for a fixed period, in exchange for regular interest payments and your original money back at the end. Bonds are generally considered lower-risk than stocks, because you're owed a specific amount rather than betting on how well a company performs. They're not risk-free, though — if whoever you lent to can't pay you back, you can still lose money.

ETFs and index funds

Picking individual stocks means researching individual companies — which is a lot of work, and easy to get wrong. Most beginner-friendly investing instead uses an index fundA fund that automatically holds a broad basket of stocks, tracking a market index — lower fees, less risk than picking single stocks., usually bought as an ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund). Instead of betting on one company, you're automatically holding a slice of hundreds or thousands of companies at once, and your result tracks the average of the whole group rather than any single company's fate.

ETF or index fund — what's the difference?

Index fund = what you ownA fund that copies a whole market list (an "index"), for example the 500 biggest US companies. You own a small piece of all of them.
ETF = how you buy itA way of packaging a fund so you can buy and sell it on the stock exchange, just like a single share.

Most beginners buy an index ETF: an index fund packaged as an ETF. So it's usually both at once, not one or the other.

Diversification

Diversification is the practical reason ETFs exist: it means spreading money across many different investments instead of concentrating it in one place, so that one company's bad year doesn't wipe out your entire investment. If you'd put all your money into a single company and it turned out to be fraudulent — like Wirecard, covered below — you'd lose everything. If that same money were spread across a thousand companies, losing one of them barely registers.

Diversification doesn't eliminate risk — the whole market can still fall. What it removes is the risk of any one company or fraud wiping you out specifically.

How to actually start

Once the vocabulary above makes sense, the practical steps are less complicated than they sound:

  1. Only invest money you won't need soon. Investments can lose value in the short term — money you need for rent or bills next month doesn't belong here.
  2. Use a licensed, regulated broker or platform. Whoever you invest through should be registered with your country's financial regulator — this is the single most important defence against fraud, covered below in Red flags.
  3. Start with a broad index fund/ETF, not individual stocks. It's the most diversified, lowest-effort starting point, for the reasons above.
  4. Expect to hold for years, not weeks. The next section shows why time matters more than timing.

Why prices move, and why time matters more than timing

Why prices move — a real case study

Click each marker on the chart below. It shows the share price of Wirecard AG, a German payments company, from 2015 to its collapse in 2020. The labelled prices are real; the line between them shows the general direction.

2015 to September 2018 — the climb, despite warnings. By September 2018 the share price had climbed to almost €200. Wirecard's share price climbed for years and the company joined Germany's DAX 30 (its 30 largest listed companies) in 2018 — even though journalists and short-sellers had been publicly questioning its accounting since 2015. Most investors and Germany's financial regulator dismissed those warnings; the regulator investigated the journalists raising concerns rather than the company itself.
2019 — the warnings get louder. The Financial Times published reports questioning Wirecard's accounting, and the share price dropped sharply. Instead of investigating the company, Germany's financial regulator banned bets against Wirecard's shares and filed a complaint against the journalists. Many investors took that as a sign the company was safe.
18 June 2020 — the crash. Wirecard announced that auditors could not verify €1.9 billion in cash the company claimed to hold in Philippine bank accounts. Shares fell more than 60% in a single day. On 25 June, a week after the announcement, the company filed for insolvency. By 26 June, nine days after the €104.50 price on 17 June, shares had fallen by about 99% to a closing low of €1.30. Its CEO was arrested and its COO fled the country and remains a fugitive.

The lesson isn't "one company lied." The warning signs were public for five years before the collapse. Prices don't only move on news — sometimes they move when reality finally catches up to warnings everyone had already dismissed.

The compound interest calculator

This shows 1,000 growing at a steady 5% a year. You don't need to type anything: just drag the slider to choose how many years the money stays invested, and watch the result change.

What is an "annual return"? It's how much your money grows in one year, as a percentage. A 5% annual return means 100 becomes 105 after one year. In the next year you earn 5% on the whole 105, not just the original 100. That's compound interest.

1,000 → 2,079

Example: if you invest 1,000 today and it grows 5% a year, after 15 years you would have about 2,079, without adding any more money. That's 1,079 you never had to pay in.

Red flags

How to spot a scam before it spots you

Most financial fraud isn't invisible until it's too late — the warning signs are usually there early. The hard part is not dismissing them.

Warnings that get ignored

Wirecard's collapse, covered above, wasn't sudden — even though the price crash was. Journalists and short-sellers publicly questioned its accounting as early as 2015, five years before the company folded. Germany's financial regulator largely dismissed those warnings, and at one point investigated the journalists raising them instead of the company itself.

The lesson generalises: warning signs are usually visible well before a collapse. They get dismissed because the company or investment looks big, official, or successful — and "everyone already trusts it" quietly replaces actual evidence.

Why young investors are exposed

This isn't a hypothetical risk. A large US survey found a real, current pattern: 26% of investors use recommendations from social media "finfluencers" when making investment decisions — and among investors under 35, 61% do (frequently or sometimes).

The same survey shows why confidence can be dangerous. Investors under 35 answered, on average, 4.3 of 11 basic investing questions correctly and 4.3 incorrectly. Among investors who rated themselves as highly knowledgeable, 51% could not identify the warning signs of fraud. And when asked whether they would invest in an offer promising a "guaranteed, risk-free 25% annual return," half of all investors said yes — 64% of those aged 18 to 34.

The survey covers US adults who already invest, so it doesn't measure teenagers directly. Treat it as a picture of the people you're about to become.

Source: FINRA Investor Education Foundation, Investors in the United States: A Report of the National Financial Capability Study, 4th edition, December 2025 — pages 1, 26–27, 30–32. 2,861 investors surveyed in 2024; answers are self-reported (p. 34).

Glossary

Every term on this site, in plain language

Alphabetical. If a word shows up anywhere on this site and you're not sure what it means, it's probably here.

Asset
Anything you own that has value and could be sold — cash, stocks, bonds, property.
Bear market
A period when prices across the market are broadly falling, usually by 20% or more from a recent high.
Bond
A loan you make to a company or government, in exchange for regular interest payments and your original money back at a set date.
Broker
A licensed platform or company you use to actually buy and sell investments. Should always be registered with your country's financial regulator.
Bull market
A period when prices across the market are broadly rising.
Compound interest
Interest calculated on both your original amount and on interest you've already earned — meaning growth accelerates over time rather than staying flat. See the calculator above.
Diversification
Spreading money across many different investments so that one company's bad year, or one fraud, can't wipe out everything you have.
Dividend
A portion of a company's profit paid out directly to shareholders, rather than reinvested into the company.
ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund)
A fund that trades like a single stock but actually holds many investments at once — the most common way beginners access diversification and index funds.
Finfluencer
Someone who gives financial or investing advice on social media, often without any licence or accountability if that advice turns out to be wrong.
Fraud
Deliberate deception for financial gain — like Wirecard fabricating billions in cash that never existed.
Index fund
A fund that automatically holds a broad basket of stocks, tracking a market index — lower fees, less risk than picking single stocks.
Inflation
The general rise in prices over time, which reduces how much your money can buy if it isn't growing at least as fast.
Insolvency
The point at which a company can no longer pay what it owes — often the end stage of a company collapsing, as with Wirecard in June 2020.
Liquidity
How quickly and easily an investment can be turned back into cash without losing value in the process.
Market capitalization ("market cap")
The total value of a company's shares — share price multiplied by the number of shares that exist.
Portfolio
The full collection of investments a person or fund holds, considered as a whole rather than one at a time.
Risk tolerance
How much loss in value someone can accept without panicking or needing the money back — different for everyone, and worth being honest with yourself about.
Short-seller
An investor who profits when a stock's price falls, typically by betting against companies they believe are overvalued or fraudulent. Short-sellers were among the first to publicly question Wirecard.
Stock (share)
A small ownership stake in a company. Owning stock means your money's value rises and falls with the company's fortunes.
Volatility
How much and how quickly a price moves up and down. High volatility means bigger swings in both directions, not just downward risk.
